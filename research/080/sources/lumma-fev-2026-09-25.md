**This is the first artifact in the record whose base model was pretrained from scratch for this
purpose rather than adapted from a general open model.** decider builds on Qwen3.5, imajev builds on
Qwen3.5, AnyJev reads off whatever host you give it. Lumma's 0.6B backbone is its own 1T-token
pretrain with shared-KV attention and an Indic-heavy mixture, and the decision model is a LoRA on
top, merged. Whether that buys anything is exactly what an outside run can answer.

## The headline is a Banking77 number, and the training data is not disclosed

| Benchmark | Jev 1.13.0 | Laya | Lumma-Fev-0.15B | Lumma-Fev-0.6B |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Banking77 | 0.87 | 0.425 | 0.47 | **0.90** |
| DAIR Emotion | 0.48 | 0.595 | 0.68 | **0.89** |
| AG News | 0.91 | **0.95** | 0.89 | 0.85 |
| Typed-decisions | 0.72 | **0.76** | 0.2 | 0.3 |
| Average | **0.75** | 0.68 | 0.56 | 0.74 |

**The card states no fine-tuning data at all.** There is no mixture, no source list, no
contamination statement, and no datasheet in the repository's file list — only `modeling_fev.py`,
`configuration_fev.py`, the tokenizer and the weights. So a 0.90 on Banking77 against Jev's 0.87
cannot be read as held-out, and it cannot be read as contaminated either. **It is unknown, which is
a different disposition from both**, and it is recorded that way rather than guessed in either
direction.

Two things in the table deserve saying plainly. Its own average of 0.74 is **below** the Jev 0.75 it
is tabulated against, so the headline win is one row of four. And it reports **0.3 on
typed-decisions against Jev's 0.72** — a bad number, published, in the same table as the good one,
which is more than most cards in this record do.

## What is good here

- **Apache-2.0 on the weights**, so it can be scored by us rather than quoted.
- **The option ceiling is 255, not 26.** AnyJev's letter readout caps a decision at 26 options and
  cannot express BANKING77 as one question; a pointer readout over option positions can. That is
  the same architectural question answered two different ways, and M5's T2a is a direct test.
- **Question independence is stated as a property**: "Questions never see each other… one question
  cannot change another's answer", and text in the request cannot forge delimiter tokens. That is a
  prompt-injection boundary claimed at the interface level rather than bolted on.
- **Its limits section says the confidences are the model's own estimates and tells you to measure
  calibration on your own labelled data before gating automated actions on them.** That is the
  vibecheck hazard stated by the vendor, unprompted.

## Disposition

Worth running, with `provenance: undisclosed` on every row. It is cheap — 649M, Apache-2.0, serves
the wire format we already drive — and it answers three questions nothing else in the table does:
whether a purpose-pretrained backbone beats an adapted general one at this size, whether a pointer
readout is order-invariant where a letter readout is not, and what a 0.90 Banking77 claim looks like
on 6,000 rows we partitioned ourselves.

A 4B and a 9B are announced for 2026-09-26 with a blog. Revisit then, with the same scrutiny — and
if the mixture is published there, this card's central gap closes.

## Run notes, 2026-09-25: measured on M5, and a pointer readout is not order-invariant

Revision `9f12b0cc`, L4, one shipped `model.decide()` call per row, zero-shot, fit examples unused.
All 78,845 rows, every emitted string asserted to be in `options_enumerated`, zero violations.

**The 255-option ceiling is real and it matters.** `modeling_fev.py` line 29 sets
`MAX_OPTIONS = 255` and `pack()` enforces it, so T2a's 77 options went in as **one question with no
partitioning** — the thing AnyJev's 26-letter readout cannot do at all. Two readout designs, one
architectural question, and the pointer head wins it outright.

Then it loses the other one.

| task | options | Lumma flip | AnyJev L0 flip | imajev flip (rotations 1) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| T2a | 77 | 0.1267 | 0.2467 (partitioned) | 0.110 |
| T2b | 2 | **0.2533** | **0.0000** | 0.047 |
| T2c | 2 | 0.0033 | 0.0000 | 0.063 |

**A quarter of its binary answers change when the two options are reversed.** The mechanism is
visible in its own code: `PointerHead` compares the hidden state at `<decide>` against each
`</opt>` in a single causal row, so options sit at different positions behind different prefixes.
Order is a real input to this architecture, not a symmetry it happens to break. AnyJev pays cyclic
shifts to get exact invariance at two options; imajev ships `--rotations 4` to damp the same effect;
Lumma pays nothing and reports nothing, and the price shows up here.

T2c's 0.0033 is not evidence against this. That task's output is nearly degenerate — see below —
and a near-constant answer cannot flip.

### What it actually emitted

- **T2b: 91.7% `out of scope`** against a 5.8% true rate, with a mean max-probability of 0.624 on a
  two-option question, which is near chance. With a 0.2533 flip rate beside it, this arm is not
  performing T2b in any meaningful sense.
- **T2c: 0.475% `toxic`** against a true 8.4%, under a cost matrix that prices a false negative at
  four times a false positive. Near-constant `not toxic` is the worst available behaviour there.
- **T2a: all 77 labels used, largest class 151 of 6,000.** That looks like real discrimination, and
  T2a is the task its card makes its headline claim about.

Four arms have now missed T2b's 5.8% base rate in four different ways: Lumma over-calls by sixteen
times, imajev by about seven, AnyJev L0 by about five, R4-gliner2 under-calls by half. **Whatever
base-rate calibration is, no artifact form in this experiment gets it from the spec.**

### The rejected optimization is the methodological point

A padded-batch path was built, validated and **thrown away** because it disagreed with the shipped
`decide()` on 4 of 300 T2b rows — bf16 batch-shape noise flipping near-ties. Four in 300 is 0.013,
the same order as a flip rate, so a four-fold speedup would have contaminated the measurement it was
meant to accelerate. Everything ran one row at a time through the shipped path.

That is the correct call and it generalizes: **an optimization whose error is the size of the effect
is not an optimization.** It is worth stating beside the serving substitutions that were accepted
today — batched teacher generation, gradient accumulation, replica concurrency — all of which were
kept precisely because they were verified to change no answer.

### Also recorded

Reclaimed L4 at 49 rows short of T2c, recovered from 3.5-minute off-VM snapshots and resumed by
confirmation id on a T4, where throughput fell from 25.7 to 8.96 rows/s because Turing has no bf16
