# arbiter — card (2026-09-25)

`0xBakeer/arbiter`, created 2026-09-20, 62 commits, 27 stars, **MIT**; the Laya weights it serves
are Apache-2.0 from Convai Innovations. Read from the README; nothing installed or run. Every
number is **Reported**.

A serving layer for typed-decision models, deliberately not a model: an HTTP server speaking
TypeSafe's Jev contract, cross-request micro-batching, routing between three Laya checkpoints, a
playground, MCP and Claude Code integrations, and **one install-and-serve recipe per accelerator**.

With `decider` (a model family) and `vibecheck` (a client DSL), this is the third layer of the same
stack sighted in two days, from three unrelated authors. That convergence is itself worth noting in
§3.6: the typed-decision call shape is being rebuilt independently at the model, the server and the
client.

## It is the §3.7 argument, already built by someone else

Plan v4 §3.7 says the compute envelope is a factor to sample rather than a constraint to declare,
and that a recipe must carry the envelope it was measured on. arbiter does exactly that, without
being asked to: `recipes/nvidia` and `recipes/apple`, each with **its own measurements and its own
chosen dtype**, plus an opt-in MLX engine on the Apple lane. The device is detected rather than
configured.

| Machine | 1 question | 50 questions | throughput at 8 callers |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| GB10, card idle | 20.9 ms | 152.4 ms | 287 q/s |
| GB10, beside a busy LLM | 326.6 ms | 381.6 ms | 88 q/s |
| Apple M2 Max, fp32 | 30.3 ms | 462.3 ms | 102 q/s |
| Apple M2 Max, `laya_mlx` | 22.7 ms | 448.4 ms | 103 q/s |

Two details are more useful than the headline numbers. **Sharing a card is not free in the
direction people forget**: while a co-resident LLM decodes at 92% utilisation, a single-question
call goes from 20.9 ms to 326.6 ms and throughput roughly halves — with no errors, so nothing
surfaces except latency. And the MLX engine's value is mostly **cold load, 2.1 s against 88–101 s**,
which matters for a laptop and not at all for a server.

## The finding that bears directly on M5's T2a

> "A 77-option question like Banking77 therefore gets about 3–4 tokens per label and accuracy
> collapses — 0.425, against 0.870 for Jev. Above about 125 options the English checkpoint cannot
> fit the markers at all, and this server returns `422` rather than silently answering a truncated
> question."

This is a **mechanism**, not a score. An encoder-with-option-markers spends its sequence budget on
the options themselves — 192 of 512 tokens for English — so a high-cardinality Choice starves both
the labels and the state. M5's T2a is BANKING77, 77-way, and the design lock already predicts it is
likely unpowered on variance grounds. This adds a second, independent reason to expect trouble
there, and it is a reason about the **artifact form** rather than about the task: a rung that
encodes options in the prompt has a cardinality ceiling that a logistic head over embeddings does
not.

Returning `422` rather than answering a truncated question is the right behaviour and worth
borrowing as guard text.

## Two more things worth taking

**A worked refusal to trade calibration for speed.** `ARBITER_DTYPE=bf16` is 15–35% faster and is
*not* the default, because against the SDK reference it moves reported probabilities by up to
**2.1e-2** and flips one argmax on a five-level score whose own confidence was 0.157. The author's
reasoning: "Laya's proposition is calibrated probabilities... spending 2e-2 of that budget to save
six milliseconds is the wrong trade." `graphs` mode carries 1.4e-3 where eager carries none, and
that is reported rather than rounded away. This is the clearest third-party example of pricing a
numerical deviation against what the artifact claims to provide, and it belongs in the skill's
guidance on serving settings.

**Calibration is a separate step, measured.** The checkpoints "ship over-confident", and refitting
one temperature **per (question type, option count)** moves mean ECE from 0.466 to 0.081 for
English and 0.314 to 0.106 for multilingual. That is direct support for R1's registered OOF
temperature step, and the per-option-count grouping is a refinement we do not currently make.

Also: the English checkpoint "does not degrade gently off English, it collapses — 0.100 on
20-option Hindi intent classification against 0.050 for guessing — and it stays confident while
doing it." Another dated instance of confident-where-wrong, alongside decider's 0.30 hard-item ECE.

## Honest about what it is not

The card quotes the model's limits rather than the server's, and leads with the unflattering one:
the base checkpoints are **near chance zero-shot on typed decisions** — 0.362 English, 0.352
multilingual, against 0.318 random and 0.461 always-majority — and the 0.766 figure belongs to the
checkpoint fine-tuned on that benchmark's own training split. "Laya is a fast base to specialise,
not a zero-shot decision engine." Ordinal `score` is called the weakest primitive at 0.372 on
SST-5. The games showcase says plainly it demonstrates speed and typed decisions, "not a claim
about intelligence", and reports where the model loses to a hand-written baseline.

## Disposition

**Discovery record, and prior art for §3.7. Not an M5 arm and not a comparator.** Nothing here was
run by us, and the typed-decisions checkpoint is fine-tuned on a benchmark's own training split,
which is the contamination shape the M5 gate already names.

What it changes: it gives an independent, measured instance of the per-envelope recipe pattern the
trainer skill is being built around, and it supplies a mechanism — the option-token budget — for a
difficulty M5's T2a was already expected to have. Both are worth citing rather than rediscovering.
